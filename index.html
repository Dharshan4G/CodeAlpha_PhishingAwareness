Step 1

What is phishing?

Phishing is a cyberattack where criminals pretend to be someone you trust, like your bank, your boss, a delivery company or IT support. They want you to hand over passwords, money or access, or to open a file that installs malware.

It works because it targets people, not technology. The best firewall in the world can't stop you from typing your password into a fake page. According to Verizon's Data Breach Investigations Reports, the human element (mistakes, stolen credentials and social engineering) is involved in the majority of breaches year after year.

Email phishingMass emails sent to thousands, hoping a few people click.
Spear phishingTailored to one person, using their name, role or recent activity.
WhalingSpear phishing aimed at executives and senior staff.
SmishingPhishing by SMS or WhatsApp, such as fake parcel or bank alerts.
VishingVoice calls from fake banks, police or tech support.
Business email compromiseFake "CEO" or supplier emails requesting urgent payments.
Clone phishingA copy of a real email you received, with the link swapped.
QuishingMalicious QR codes on posters, parking meters or emails.

Step 3 · Interactive

Spot the fake website

Fake sites copy the look of real ones perfectly, so the address bar is where you catch them. Read a URL by finding the real domain: the part just before the first single slash / after https://. Read that part right to left.

Remember that the padlock icon only means the connection is encrypted. It says nothing about who owns the site. Most phishing sites use HTTPS today.

Is each address real or fake?

    Other signs of a fake site

    • The page asks for more than the real site ever would, such as your card PIN, full card number or one-time codes.
    • Low-quality logos, spelling errors, or links in the footer that don't work.
    • You arrived from a link in an email or text instead of typing the address yourself.
    • Your password manager doesn't autofill. It matches exact domains, so it won't be fooled by a lookalike.
    Step 4

    Social engineering: how they hack your mind

    Attackers exploit normal human instincts. If a message makes you feel a strong emotion and pushes you to act fast, slow down.

    Urgency

    A deadline leaves you no time to think.Your account will be closed in 2 hours.

    Authority

    They pose as a boss, police, government or IT department, and people rarely question authority.This is the CEO. I need this wire transfer done before my meeting.

    Fear

    They threaten fines, arrest, hacked accounts or lost data.We found illegal files on your computer. Pay the fine now.

    Greed and reward

    They offer prizes, refunds or easy money.Congratulations! You've won an iPhone. Pay only the shipping.

    Curiosity

    They tempt you with a document or link you want to see.Salary_Revisions_2026.xlsx — confidential.

    Trust and helpfulness

    They pose as a colleague or friend who needs a small favour.Hey, I'm locked out. Can you read me the code you just got?

    Pretexting

    They build a believable backstory first, often over several messages or calls.Hi, I'm from the IT help desk, following up on the ticket about your slow laptop…

    Baiting and tailgating

    These are physical tricks. An infected USB stick is left in the car park labelled "Payroll", or someone follows staff through a secure door carrying boxes.
    Step 5

    Real attacks that started with one message

    2013–15

    Google and Facebook lose over $100 million

    A Lithuanian man set up a fake company named after Quanta Computer, a real hardware supplier. He emailed fake invoices to the finance teams of both tech giants, and they paid for about two years. He was caught and sentenced to five years in US prison.

    Lesson: always confirm payment or bank-detail changes through a separate, known channel, even for big "trusted" suppliers.

    2016

    A political campaign's emails leak

    John Podesta, chairman of Hillary Clinton's presidential campaign, received a fake "someone has your password" Google alert. The link led to a lookalike login page, and thousands of his emails were later published.

    Lesson: security alerts are a favourite disguise. Go to the site yourself instead of clicking the link.

    2020

    Twitter's celebrity accounts hijacked

    Attackers phoned Twitter employees pretending to be internal IT and steered them to a fake VPN login page. With the stolen access, they posted Bitcoin scams from the accounts of Barack Obama, Elon Musk, Apple and others.

    Lesson: vishing works. Verify callers by calling them back on an official number.

    2023

    MGM Resorts shut down by a phone call

    Attackers found an employee on LinkedIn and called MGM's IT help desk pretending to be that person. They talked their way into a login reset. The ransomware attack that followed disrupted hotels and casinos for days and cost the company around $100 million.

    Lesson: help desks need strict identity checks, and the information you share publicly can be used against your employer.

    Step 6

    How to protect yourself

    • Stop, think, verify. If a message is urgent, emotional or unexpected, pause before acting.
    • Hover before you click. Check where a link really goes. On a phone, press and hold the link.
    • Go direct. Type the website address yourself or use the official app, never the link in the message.
    • Verify through another channel. Call the person or company on a number you already know, not one given in the message.
    • Turn on multi-factor authentication (MFA) everywhere. Passkeys and security keys resist phishing best.
    • Never share one-time codes. No real bank, company or IT team will ever ask for them.
    • Use a password manager. It won't autofill on fake domains, and it lets you use a unique password for every site.
    • Be careful with attachments, especially .exe, .zip, .html, .iso, and Office files that ask you to "enable macros".
    • Keep software updated so a click can't exploit old vulnerabilities.
    • Limit what you share publicly. Your job title, manager's name and holiday plans all make spear phishing easier.
    • Report it. Use your organisation's "Report phishing" button. In the UK you can forward emails to report@phishing.gov.uk and texts to 7726.

    Already clicked? Act fast. It isn't a disaster if you act quickly.

    1. Disconnect from the network if you opened an attachment or installed something.
    2. Change the password you entered, from a different device, along with any account that reuses it.
    3. Contact your bank immediately if you shared card or payment details.
    4. Tell your IT or security team right away. Early reports limit the damage.
    5. Watch your accounts for unusual activity over the following weeks.
